The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

Does technology make us less intelligent, or does it change what human intelligence can accomplish? And what becomes possible when human intelligence is amplified by artificial intelligence?

Every generation seems to worry that the next generation is losing its intellectual abilities. Writing was once criticized for weakening memory. Calculators reduced the need for mental arithmetic. Search engines reduced the need to memorize facts. GPS reduced the need to navigate independently. Now, artificial intelligence is accused of making us incapable of thinking for ourselves.

The concern is understandable. Students ask AI to solve assignments, programmers generate code they cannot explain, and people increasingly turn to chatbots for answers they might otherwise have worked out independently. But beneath this criticism lies an assumption worth examining: if technology lets us accomplish something without exercising the cognitive ability previously required, does that mean we have become less intelligent?

Perhaps the more useful question is not whether AI will replace human thinking. It is whether AI can expand the boundaries of what humans can learn, create, and discover.

1. What Do We Mean by “Less Intelligent”?

Imagine two people trying to remember a telephone number. One repeatedly recalls it until it is memorized. The other saves it in a contact list and retrieves it whenever necessary. After a month, the first person may remember the number perfectly while the second may not remember it at all.

We have measured a difference in recall for one particular piece of information. We have not measured their general memory capacity, reasoning ability, ability to learn new information, or capacity to solve unfamiliar problems.

Three outcomes should be kept separate:

  • Independent capability: what a person can remember, understand, or do without assistance.
  • Augmented capability: what that person can accomplish using their mind together with external tools.
  • Learning and transfer: whether using a tool helps them solve new problems independently in the future.

These outcomes can diverge. A person can become more productive without learning more. Another person can use feedback from a tool to develop stronger skills. A third may become effective with assistance but struggle when the tool is unavailable.

2. The Phone Book Fallacy: What Happened to Our Memory?

Human memory is not a passive storage device. It involves encoding, consolidation, retrieval, and forgetting. Retrieval practice—trying to recall information rather than merely rereading it—can strengthen long-term retention. Spacing those attempts over time can help as well.

In a 2006 paper, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke showed that retrieval practice could improve long-term retention compared with repeated studying alone. This helps explain why a person who repeatedly recalls a number may remember it better than someone who always retrieves it from a phone.

But that mechanism does not prove that smartphones have caused a general decline in human memory. It shows why the need to remember particular information can decline when reliable external storage is available.

In 2011, Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu, and Daniel Wegner published Google Effects on Memory. Their experiments suggested that when people expected information to remain accessible, they were less likely to recall the information itself and more likely to remember where it could be found. The result has important limitations, including a later replication that did not reproduce the original effect under its specified criteria.

The broader idea of cognitive offloading remains useful: notebooks, search engines, calculators, and digital storage let us move some information-processing demands outside the brain. This can free attention for other tasks, but it can also mean we fail to practise skills we still need.

3. From Calculators to AI: A Change in the Scale of Delegation

Earlier technologies often automated specific operations: calculation, storage, retrieval, measurement, and navigation. Generative AI can participate in more complex activities, including explanation, programming, summarization, hypothesis generation, and iterative problem-solving.

A calculator performs an operation that the user specifies. An AI assistant can help formulate a problem, propose a method, implement the calculation, explain an unexpected result, and suggest what to investigate next. It can contribute at several stages of a task.

This does not mean AI always reasons correctly or understands a problem as a human does. It means AI can participate in more of the workflow. Someone who copies an AI-generated solution may complete a task without learning it. Someone who uses AI to compare derivations, test hypotheses, and investigate errors may develop a stronger understanding.

The important question is not only what we delegate, but what we do with the time and attention that delegation frees.

4. The Evidence We Must Take Seriously

AI can encourage shallow engagement

In 2025, researchers including Nataliya Kosmyna and Pattie Maes published Your Brain on ChatGPT, a study of people writing essays with an LLM, using a search engine, or working without external tools. The researchers reported differences in measured brain connectivity and concerns about essay ownership and recall among the LLM group.

This raises a serious question: if AI produces much of the work, are people engaging less deeply with the material? But the study involved a relatively small sample and a specific essay-writing task. EEG connectivity is not a direct measure of intelligence, and the results do not establish that AI generally causes lasting cognitive damage.

AI can also improve learning

A 2025 randomized trial by Greg Kestin and colleagues compared an AI-powered physics tutor with an active-learning classroom lesson. Under the tested conditions, students using the AI tutor learned more in less time. The tutor was designed around research-informed pedagogical principles rather than simply providing unrestricted answers.

The two studies are not contradictory. They examine different kinds of AI use. A system that replaces the learner's effort may have different consequences from one that structures explanations and practice to help the learner understand.

A useful distinction: using AI to bypass the learning process is not the same as using AI to improve the learning process. Neither outcome should be assumed from the mere presence of AI.

5. Productivity Is Not the Same as Intelligence

In a 2023 randomized experiment, Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang studied 453 college-educated professionals performing writing tasks. Participants given ChatGPT completed tasks about 40% faster, while the quality of their output increased by about 18% on the study's measures.

That is evidence of improved task performance under the conditions tested. It is not proof that participants became more intelligent. Productivity, independent expertise, and long-term learning are different outcomes.

A related 2025 analysis of three field experiments involving 4,867 software developers estimated an average increase of about 26% in completed tasks for developers using AI coding assistance, although effects varied across experiments. Again, completing more tasks does not automatically mean developing deeper programming expertise.

Still, these findings show why the debate cannot be reduced to whether AI replaces effort. AI can change the cost of implementation, which changes what people can attempt within a given amount of time.

6. When AI Can Make Us Worse at Thinking

If students repeatedly delegate reasoning, they may fail to develop foundational skills. If programmers accept generated code without understanding it, they may become less capable of debugging independently. If researchers accept plausible explanations without verification, errors can propagate.

Fluency is not correctness. An explanation can sound convincing while containing a false claim, a missing assumption, or a faulty derivation. Understanding an explanation while reading it is not the same as reproducing the reasoning independently.

Research on learning also shows that practice that feels more demanding—such as retrieving information from memory—can improve long-term retention. So we should preserve the effort that produces learning rather than remove every difficulty.

  • Attempt a problem before asking AI for a solution.
  • Ask for hints and feedback before requesting a complete answer.
  • Verify claims, assumptions, code, and calculations.
  • Explain the result in your own words.
  • Return later and solve a related problem without assistance.

The objective is not to avoid AI. It is to use it without confusing a completed task with a learned skill.

7. From Completing Problems to Attempting New Ones

Now consider a possibility that receives less attention. What if AI's most important contribution is not helping us solve the same problems faster, but enabling us to attempt problems we previously could not afford to investigate?

Imagine a researcher with an idea for a new neural-network architecture but insufficient time to implement it. AI might help prototype the idea, generate tests, identify implementation errors, and compare alternatives. The architecture may still fail, and the results still need careful evaluation. But reducing the cost of experimentation can make more hypotheses testable.

A technology that makes an existing task faster improves efficiency. A technology that makes previously impractical tasks feasible can expand the range of problems people are able to explore.

This distinction matters for science. Discovery depends not only on the quality of ideas, but also on the cost of testing them. If AI helps researchers examine more candidate explanations within the same budget, it may increase the number of ideas that can be investigated. That is an opportunity, not a guarantee of discovery: hypotheses still need evidence, and experiments still need to be valid and reproducible.

8. Where Does Human Intelligence End?

In 1998, Andy Clark and David Chalmers published The Extended Mind, challenging the assumption that cognition must occur entirely inside the brain. A notebook can serve as external memory; a mathematical tool can help manipulate relationships; a computer can simulate a system too complicated to calculate by hand.

AI introduces a more complex possibility. Unlike a notebook, it can transform information, generate candidate explanations, produce code, and participate in iterative problem-solving. This does not mean that AI and the human brain are identical, or that every interaction with AI should be regarded as part of a person's mind. Extended cognition is a theoretical framework, not proof that human intelligence increases whenever AI is used.

But it gives us a useful way to think about a combined system. The human contributes goals, context, judgment, and responsibility. AI contributes capabilities that may reduce the cost of certain operations. The human evaluates the results and decides what to do next.

When these contributions complement one another, the combined system may accomplish something that would be difficult for either component to accomplish independently.

Greater effective intelligence does not necessarily imply greater intrinsic intelligence.

9. What a More AI-Enabled Human Future Could Look Like

The implications may extend beyond individual productivity. AI could change who has access to intellectual capability and which ideas people can turn into action. The possibilities below have different levels of evidence; they are opportunities to develop and evaluate, not guaranteed outcomes.

Education: a personal tutor for every learner

An AI tutor could explain a concept at different levels, generate practice problems, identify misconceptions, and adapt to a learner's progress. A student struggling with calculus could begin with geometric intuition and gradually move toward formal derivations. High-quality individualized instruction could become more accessible, provided that systems are accurate, pedagogically sound, and designed to encourage independent understanding.

Science: more hypotheses and more experiments

AI could help researchers search literature, generate candidate hypotheses, write analysis code, design simulations, and prioritize experiments. This could reduce the cost of testing ideas and allow researchers to investigate more possibilities. Scientific validation, reproducibility, and independent review would remain essential.

Healthcare: expanding access to expertise

AI may help clinicians summarize records, interpret information, and reduce repetitive administrative work. It may help people understand medical information and prepare questions for professionals. The goal should be better access and support—not replacing clinical responsibility. Accuracy, privacy, bias, and appropriate oversight remain critical.

Accessibility: more people can participate

AI can help convert speech to text, describe visual information, summarize documents, translate languages, and adapt digital interfaces. These capabilities may reduce barriers to education, work, communication, and daily life. Their value depends on reliability, affordability, and thoughtful design for actual users.

Creativity: turning ideas into working systems

A teacher might build an interactive learning tool. A scientist might prototype a visualization. A hobbyist might create a small application that once required extensive programming experience. AI can lower the cost of experimentation and help people move from intention to implementation. The results still need evaluation, and generated content is not automatically original or high quality.

Language and knowledge: fewer barriers to learning

Translation and interactive explanations could help people access research and education beyond the languages they know well. This may broaden participation in intellectual work, provided that systems preserve meaning, uncertainty, and cultural context rather than merely producing fluent text.

Expertise: helping knowledge reach further

AI may reduce the expertise needed to begin a technical project, even when substantial expertise remains necessary to verify, complete, and deploy it responsibly. More people may be able to formulate hypotheses, prototype systems, analyze data, and test ideas. Experts remain important because they can judge which results are meaningful and which are wrong.

10. The Risks of a More Capable World

A brighter future is not inevitable. If AI systems are inaccurate, expensive, inaccessible, or controlled by a small number of institutions, their benefits may be unevenly distributed. If people rely on AI without understanding its limitations, errors can spread more quickly. If education rewards only completed assignments, students may optimize for answers rather than understanding.

There is also no guarantee that productivity gains will translate into better lives. A technology can increase output without reducing working hours, improving wages, or expanding access to essential services.

These are reasons to think carefully about how benefits are distributed and risks are managed. The objective should not be to maximize dependence on AI. It should be to build a world in which AI expands human opportunity while people retain meaningful agency and the ability to evaluate the systems they use.

11. How Should We Measure the Future of Human Intelligence?

The debate needs better measurements than task completion alone. Research should distinguish independent capability, performance with AI, delayed retention, transfer to unfamiliar problems, and the ability to identify errors in AI-generated work.

These outcomes can diverge. A student might become more productive without learning more. Another might use AI feedback to acquire skills faster. A third might become effective with AI but struggle when it is unavailable.

Carefully designed studies should investigate long-term effects across different subjects, ages, expertise levels, and patterns of use. The goal is not to prove in advance that AI makes us smarter or dumber. It is to discover which interactions improve human capability, which undermine it, and how to design better systems.

12. Conclusion: Intelligence Is More Than Unaided Mental Effort

Human progress has never required us to perform every task with our unaided minds. Writing preserves ideas beyond immediate memory. Calculators allow us to explore mathematical problems without manually performing every arithmetic operation. Scientific instruments reveal phenomena that unaided perception cannot detect.

These technologies can reduce the need to practise certain skills, but they can also expand what people can accomplish. AI introduces a further possibility: delegating some cognitive operations while retaining human judgment and expanding the range of questions we can investigate.

The risks are real. People can become dependent on tools, fail to practise important skills, or accept answers they do not understand. But it would be equally premature to conclude that AI inevitably makes people less intelligent. Research on AI-assisted tutoring and professional work shows that carefully designed systems can also improve learning or productivity under particular conditions.

The more useful question is not whether AI performs some of our thinking for us. It is which kinds of thinking we should retain, which operations we can delegate, and how we can use the resulting capabilities to learn more deeply and solve more difficult problems.

A world where a curious student can access individualized instruction, a researcher can test more hypotheses, a person with a disability can overcome barriers to participation, and an ordinary individual can turn an idea into a working system is a world with greater intellectual opportunity.

None of this is guaranteed. It depends on the quality of AI systems, the way institutions are designed, and whether we preserve the human capabilities needed to guide and evaluate them. But the possibility deserves to be taken seriously.

Perhaps the goal of intelligence is not to perform every operation ourselves. Perhaps it is to understand the world, decide which problems matter, and develop the means to solve them.

And perhaps AI's greatest contribution will be to help more people do precisely that.
