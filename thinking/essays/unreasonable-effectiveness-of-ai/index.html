The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

We have mistaken doing everything ourselves for being intelligent. From writing and calculators to search engines and AI, the history of human progress tells a different story.

Imagine a person from a thousand years ago watching you work today. You want to know the distance between two cities, so you look at a map. You need to calculate a difficult sum, so you reach for a calculator. You have forgotten a fact, so you search for it. You want to speak to someone on the other side of the planet, so you take a small glass rectangle from your pocket and call them.

Would this person conclude that you are less intelligent because you do not calculate every sum in your head, memorize every road, or carry every fact in your memory? Or would they notice that you can do things that were once impossible for an ordinary person?

Now introduce artificial intelligence. You ask it to explain a difficult idea, help write a program, examine a dataset, or suggest experiments for a scientific question. Suddenly, the old objection returns. People say we are becoming lazy. Our minds are weakening. We are forgetting how to think.

I think this argument begins with the wrong picture of intelligence. It treats the human mind as if its purpose were to perform every intellectual operation alone. But the history of our species suggests something more interesting. Human beings have repeatedly changed not only what they know, but also what they can do by building tools around their minds.

AI deserves scrutiny. It can mislead us, make mistakes, and encourage shallow work. But those risks do not prove that AI is making humanity less intelligent. They show that a powerful tool can be used badly. To understand what AI might mean for us, we need to separate the loss of a particular skill from the expansion of human capability.

1. The strange idea that intelligence means doing everything yourself

Consider two people who need to multiply 847 by 936. One spends several minutes working through the arithmetic by hand. The other uses a calculator and gets the answer in seconds.

Who is more intelligent? The question is harder than it looks. If we are testing mental arithmetic, the first person has demonstrated more of that particular skill. If we are trying to discover who can use mathematics to solve a real problem before a deadline, the second person may be better equipped. If we want to know who understands the mathematical ideas behind the calculation, we need a different test entirely.

We often collapse all these abilities into one word: intelligence. Then we see a machine perform one of them and conclude that the human has lost something.

But three things are different:

  • What you can do alone. Your unaided memory, reasoning, knowledge, and practical skills.
  • What you can do with tools. The problems you can solve when your mind works with books, computers, instruments, other people, or AI.
  • What you learn along the way. Whether using those tools helps you become more capable when you face the next problem.

A calculator can increase what you accomplish without improving your mental arithmetic. A good teacher can improve both your immediate performance and your future ability. An AI assistant can do either, depending on how you use it.

The important point is that a decline in one unaided skill does not automatically mean a decline in intelligence as a whole. We must ask what was lost, what was gained, and what the person can now accomplish.

2. We did not stop thinking when we stopped memorizing everything

For most of human history, memory was not merely useful. It was essential. Knowledge that was not remembered could disappear. Stories carried history. Apprentices memorized procedures. Scholars trained themselves to retain enormous quantities of text. The human mind had to serve as both the thinker and much of the storage system.

Writing changed this arrangement. A thought could outlive the person who first had it. Knowledge could travel across generations without every detail being memorized by every individual. A reader could build on the work of people they would never meet.

Writing did not make memory useless. Nor did it make every reader wiser. But it changed the scale at which human knowledge could accumulate. A person no longer had to rebuild an entire field of knowledge from personal experience. They could begin where someone else had left off.

Books, libraries, printing, calculators, computers, and the internet continued this transformation. Each moved some burden away from the individual and into the world around them. The result was not a species that remembered everything. It was a species capable of building knowledge that no single brain could contain.

We should be careful with the claim that every new technology makes people smarter. History does not support such a simple rule. People can forget skills they no longer practise. Tools can distract as well as assist. Yet one pattern is difficult to ignore: external tools have repeatedly allowed human beings to extend their reach beyond the limits of unaided memory and calculation.

AI belongs to this history, but it introduces a new chapter. It does not merely store information or calculate what we specify. It can help transform information into explanations, code, plans, comparisons, and possible answers. The tool is beginning to participate in the work between asking a question and reaching a result.

3. The real danger is not help. It is surrender.

There is a difference between asking someone to carry you and asking someone to teach you how to climb.

A student can ask AI for the answer to a calculus problem, copy it, and move on. The assignment is finished, but the student may understand no more than before. A different student can attempt the problem, ask for a hint, compare two approaches, locate the mistake in their derivation, and then solve a similar problem without help. Both students used AI. Only one deliberately used it to learn.

This distinction matters because learning requires more than seeing a correct answer. We need to retrieve ideas, struggle with unfamiliar examples, notice mistakes, and connect new information to what we already know. If AI removes every opportunity to do that work, it can leave us with the appearance of understanding instead of understanding itself.

There is evidence that this concern deserves attention. Research on memory has repeatedly found that trying to retrieve information can strengthen long-term retention. Studies of AI-assisted writing have also raised questions about how deeply people engage with work when a system produces much of the result. These findings are reasons to design better habits and better learning tools. They are not proof that AI inevitably damages intelligence.

In fact, the same technology can be designed to do the opposite. An AI tutor can ask a learner to explain their reasoning, give a hint instead of the answer, identify a misconception, and generate a fresh problem at the right level. It can patiently explain the same idea in several ways. It can let a student practise without the embarrassment of asking a basic question in front of a room.

The difference is not between using AI and refusing to use AI. It is between using AI to escape the work of thinking and using AI to make that work more effective.

Do not ask only whether AI is doing the thinking. Ask whether the human is becoming better at thinking because of the interaction.

4. A tool that gives answers can also open doors

Imagine a student who wants to understand neural networks. The subject contains mathematics, programming, statistics, and a long trail of ideas developed by researchers over decades. A textbook may explain one step in a way the student does not understand. A search engine may return ten pages that assume the missing knowledge. A classroom may have moved on before the student has caught up.

An AI tutor can pause. It can explain a matrix as a transformation, show a small numerical example, write a short program, and then ask the student to predict what will happen when one value changes. If the explanation fails, the student can ask again from a different angle. The conversation can adapt to the learner's confusion.

This does not guarantee learning. The tutor might be wrong, and the student might accept its explanation without testing it. But it makes a form of individualized instruction easier to access. The possibility is important because access to patient, high-quality help has never been distributed equally.

A 2025 randomized study of an AI-powered physics tutor reported that students using the tutor learned more in less time than students in the comparison classroom under the conditions tested. The lesson is not that every chatbot is a good teacher. The lesson is that AI can be built to support learning rather than merely deliver answers.

When we discuss AI in education, we often imagine a student secretly asking a chatbot to complete homework. That is one possible future. Another is a student using a tireless tutor to practise, question, and understand. The technology alone does not decide which future we get. Design, incentives, and human choices matter.

5. The hidden cost of doing everything the hard way

Think about a scientist with an idea for an experiment. The idea may be promising, but testing it requires code, data cleaning, simulations, documentation, and hours of debugging. The scientist may spend so much time making the experiment run that there is little time left to explore the idea itself.

Now imagine AI helping with the repetitive parts. It drafts the first version of the code. It explains an error. It suggests tests. It helps compare alternative implementations. The scientist still has to decide what question matters, check whether the code is correct, and determine whether the results support the hypothesis. But the cost of trying the idea has fallen.

That change can matter more than a simple increase in speed. When an experiment becomes cheaper, a researcher can test ideas that once seemed too expensive to pursue. Some will fail. Some will reveal that the original question was wrong. A few may lead somewhere unexpected.

Discovery is not only a contest to have the best idea. It is also a contest to test ideas against reality. A brilliant hypothesis that cannot be investigated may remain a thought in someone's notebook. A modest hypothesis that can be tested, rejected, improved, and tested again can become knowledge.

AI could lower the cost of that process. It could help a programmer prototype a tool, a doctor organise information, a student explore a difficult concept, or a small research team examine more possibilities. It will not guarantee good results. It can, however, change which attempts are affordable.

When a tool allows us to attempt ten ideas where we could once attempt one, it has not simply replaced effort. It has changed the landscape of possibility.

6. Intelligence has always been larger than one brain

Consider a modern scientific discovery. It may depend on a researcher who asks the question, a team that builds the instrument, software that processes the data, previous studies that establish the background, and institutions that allow the work to continue. No individual possesses every skill and every fact involved. The achievement belongs to a system of people, knowledge, and tools working together.

Philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers explored a related idea in their 1998 paper The Extended Mind. They asked whether parts of the environment can sometimes function as parts of a cognitive process. A notebook that reliably stores information, for example, may do more than sit outside the mind. It can become part of the way a person remembers and reasons.

AI makes this question more interesting. A notebook preserves what we write. A calculator performs a specified operation. An AI system can propose an explanation, transform code, compare options, or help us develop a line of inquiry. It can participate in a loop: we ask, it responds, we evaluate, and we ask a better question.

That does not make the human and the machine identical. AI can produce confident nonsense. It does not take responsibility for our choices. A human must still decide what is worth pursuing and whether the result deserves trust. But the relevant unit of work may sometimes be neither the human alone nor the AI alone. It may be the combination.

We already accept that a team can accomplish more than any one member. We accept that a scientist with a microscope can discover things invisible to the unaided eye. It should not be surprising that a person working with a capable AI system may solve problems that were beyond their reach alone.

The sensible question is not whether the tool has become part of the human brain. The sensible question is whether the partnership helps people understand more, create more, and solve problems they could not previously solve.

7. Why the fear feels so convincing

There are reasons people distrust this change. The first is visible effort. When someone solves a problem by hand, we can see the work. When they use AI, much of the work seems to disappear. We are tempted to treat visible effort as evidence of intelligence and effortless success as evidence of weakness.

The second is that we confuse a tool's performance with its user's competence. If a person presents excellent code written by AI, we may not know whether they understand it. That uncertainty is real. The solution is not to pretend the concern does not exist, but to evaluate the right thing. If we need a reliable program, test the program. If we need a capable programmer, test whether the person can reason about, modify, and debug it.

The third is that the harms are easy to picture. A student copies an essay. An employee accepts a false answer. A programmer ships a bug. These are concrete stories, and they deserve attention. The benefits are often less visible. A student finally understands a concept. A researcher tests an idea that would otherwise have been abandoned. A person with a disability gains access to a task that used to be difficult or impossible.

There is also a deeper discomfort. If a machine can help with work we considered distinctly human, we may wonder what remains special about us. But human worth was never measured by how many calculations we could perform without help. Nor does the value of a musician disappear because instruments amplify the sound of their hands.

Our dignity does not depend on being the only beings capable of producing a useful answer. It depends on what we care about, the choices we make, the relationships we build, and the worlds we create together.

8. The future depends on how we use the time we gain

Technology does not automatically turn saved time into wisdom. A person who gets faster at work may simply be given more work. A student who finishes homework quickly may spend the extra time scrolling. A researcher who can generate more code may produce more experiments without asking better questions.

Efficiency is an opportunity, not an outcome. What matters is what we do with it.

We could use AI to produce more low-quality content, or to make useful knowledge easier to access. We could use it to remove all difficulty from education, or to give every learner more chances to practise. We could use it to replace human judgment, or to give people better information with which to exercise judgment.

The same tool can serve very different purposes. That is why the important debate is not simply whether AI is good or bad. It is what kind of human life we want to build around it.

9. A better way to think about intelligence

We should measure AI's effects with more care. Can people solve problems with AI that they could not solve before? Do they remember what they learned a week later? Can they transfer the knowledge to a new situation? Can they spot a wrong answer? Can they still work independently when independence matters?

These questions may produce different answers, and that is exactly why we should ask them. Productivity is not the same as learning. Learning is not the same as memory. Memory is not the same as judgment. A serious account of intelligence must make room for all of them.

We should preserve the skills that matter. Students need foundations. Programmers need to understand the systems they maintain. Researchers need to verify evidence. Everyone needs enough knowledge to notice when a tool is leading them astray. Using AI responsibly requires more than issuing instructions. It requires judgment about the answers we receive.

But preserving human skill does not require rejecting assistance. We do not ban books because students must learn to think. We do not reject calculators from every mathematical task because arithmetic practice matters. We decide which skills to practise, which tools to use, and what kind of understanding a task requires.

AI calls for the same discipline, applied to a more powerful and more flexible tool.

10. The opportunity in front of us

For most of history, deep expertise was expensive to acquire. It required access to teachers, books, institutions, equipment, and years of practice. Those requirements will not disappear. AI cannot instantly turn a beginner into a reliable surgeon or an expert scientist. Experience and responsibility still matter.

Yet AI may lower the cost of taking the first step. A person can ask questions that would once have required finding a specialist. They can test a small idea before assembling a team. They can learn enough to discover what they do not know. They can turn an idea into a prototype and use the prototype to ask a better question.

This matters because human potential is often limited not by the absence of curiosity, but by the distance between curiosity and action. A person has an idea but lacks the code. Another has the data but does not know how to analyse it. Someone wants to learn but cannot afford individual instruction. AI may help shorten these distances.

The result will not be a world in which everyone becomes an expert at everything. It may be a world in which more people can participate in work that once required resources they did not possess. That would be a meaningful expansion of human capability.

Conclusion: We do not have to think alone to think well

The fear that AI is making us less intelligent often begins with a narrow definition of intelligence. It looks at the skills we no longer need to perform unaided and overlooks the new things we may become capable of doing.

Of course, people can misuse AI. They can surrender their judgment, neglect the practice that builds expertise, and mistake fluent answers for truth. We should not dismiss these risks. A tool that can help us think can also help us avoid thinking, and we need to learn the difference.

But the existence of misuse is not proof that the tool itself diminishes us. AI can also explain, teach, challenge, translate, prototype, and help investigate questions that would otherwise remain out of reach. Early research already suggests that carefully designed AI tools can improve learning in specific settings and raise productivity in some kinds of work. The long-term effects on human capability remain an open question, not a verdict already delivered.

Human progress has never been a story of the unaided mind becoming capable of everything. It has been a story of minds joining with language, writing, mathematics, institutions, instruments, and one another. We built things that remember, calculate, reveal, and connect. Each changed what an individual could do, and what humanity could accomplish together.

AI is another step in that story, though a particularly consequential one. Its value will depend on whether we use it to replace our curiosity or to feed it; whether we accept answers or investigate them; whether we let it make decisions for us or use it to make better decisions ourselves.

Perhaps intelligence is not the ability to do everything alone. Perhaps it is the ability to discover what is possible, choose what matters, and bring together whatever helps us make it real.

If AI helps more people ask better questions, learn difficult ideas, test more possibilities, and turn more worthwhile ideas into reality, then it will not have made human intelligence irrelevant. It will have given human intelligence a wider field in which to work.

That is the future worth building, and it is a much more interesting possibility than the fear that we are simply becoming less intelligent.
