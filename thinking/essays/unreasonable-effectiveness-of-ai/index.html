The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

People say AI will make us stupid. Their strongest examples reveal a more interesting truth: tools can weaken specific skills when they replace practice, while expanding what human beings can learn and accomplish.

In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing may weaken memory and create the appearance of wisdom without the substance. More than two thousand years later, we can still read that warning because writing preserved it.

One of humanity's oldest fears about technology survived because the technology worked.

The argument has returned with AI. People point to forgotten phone numbers, students copying homework, brain scans, hallucinated answers, and generic AI writing. These are not all imaginary concerns. The mistake is turning a real risk in one kind of task into a verdict on human intelligence as a whole.

1. The phone number argument confuses practice with ability

Ten years ago, you may have remembered dozens of phone numbers. Today, you may struggle to recall even your own family's numbers.

But the old numbers were often dialled and retrieved repeatedly. The numbers stored in a phone rarely need to be recalled at all. One memory was practised. The other was outsourced.

Memory is not a permanent recording. Retrieval helps keep information accessible, and spacing those retrievals over time can strengthen long-term retention. This is why spaced repetition works.

The phone-number comparison is not a clean test of intelligence. It compares information repeatedly used with information deliberately left unused. It shows that we no longer practise memorizing numbers, not that our brains have generally become less capable.

Psychologists call the broader habit cognitive offloading: using tools or the environment to carry part of a mental task. Calendars hold appointments. Search engines hold routes to facts. A contact list holds phone numbers.

We have not lost the ability to remember a number simply because we stopped needing to remember every number.

2. “Use it or lose it” is true, but narrower than it sounds

Skills improve through practice and can weaken when practice disappears. A person who stops navigating without GPS may become less familiar with local streets. A student who copies every equation may fail to solve a similar problem alone.

This is the strongest part of the anti-AI argument, and it should not be dismissed. When AI replaces the very activity someone needs to learn, it can remove the practice that builds that skill.

But losing practice in one activity does not automatically mean losing intelligence in general. A pianist can lose finger dexterity without losing language ability. Forgetting phone numbers does not establish a decline in reasoning. The evidence must match the size of the claim.

A 2026 review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences describes genuine concerns about skill acquisition and skill decay from cognitive offloading, while treating broader changes in basic cognitive abilities as a separate and unresolved matter. The headline “AI will make everyone dumb” goes much further than that conclusion.

A tool can weaken a skill it replaces. That does not prove it weakens the whole mind.

3. “AI does the homework, so nobody learns” mistakes output for learning

A finished assignment proves that an answer was produced. It does not prove that the student understands it. This is the illusion of learning: fluent explanations can feel familiar even when the learner cannot reproduce the reasoning.

There is evidence for this risk. In a 2025 randomized trial on learning AI concepts, students who used ChatGPT as a study aid scored 57.5% on a retention test 45 days later, compared with 68.5% for students who studied without it. Only 85 of the 120 enrolled participants completed that delayed test, and the study examined one learning context, not every possible use of AI. Read the study.

Now consider a different experiment. In a 2025 randomized study of introductory physics, a purpose-built AI tutor produced more than double the median learning gains of an active-learning classroom lesson, while students spent less time on the material. The tutor was designed to teach, not simply to hand over answers. Read the study.

These experiments are not direct opposites. They used different settings and teaching designs. Together, they expose the flaw in saying that AI either destroys learning or automatically improves it. The way the tool is designed and used changes the outcome.

AI can complete the student's work, or it can help the student do the work of learning. One hides the thinking. The other supports it.

4. “AI kills critical thinking” confuses less effort with no thinking

A 2025 Microsoft Research study surveyed 319 knowledge workers and collected 936 examples of AI use. Workers who had greater confidence in AI reported less critical-thinking effort, while those more confident in their own ability reported more. The study also found that thinking often shifted from generating an answer toward checking, integrating, and supervising AI output. Read the study.

This is a warning about overtrust, not proof that AI has caused a general decline in intelligence. It was a survey of reported work experiences, not a long-term measurement of IQ or a demonstration of permanent cognitive loss.

The same distinction matters for the widely discussed MIT Media Lab study Your Brain on ChatGPT. It examined 54 participants writing essays under different conditions and measured brain activity during the task. The authors described the work as preliminary and not peer-reviewed when released. It does not establish that ChatGPT causes brain damage or makes a population less intelligent.

The real danger is passive reliance. When people accept an answer because it sounds polished, they skip the work of evaluating it. When they use AI to generate alternatives, challenge assumptions, verify sources, and explain disagreements, they are still exercising judgment.

AI can reduce the effort people invest in thinking. That is not the same claim as proving that people have lost the capacity to think.

5. “AI kills creativity” misses a startling result

In a 2024 experiment on short-story writing, people given AI-generated ideas produced stories that were rated as more creative and enjoyable, particularly writers who started out less creative. But those AI-assisted stories were also more similar to one another. Read the study in Science Advances.

AI improved individual results while narrowing the variety of results across the group. That is a much more revealing problem than saying that AI simply destroys creativity.

The risk is convergence. If millions of people begin with the same suggestions and accept the first polished answer, the world may fill with competent work that resembles itself. Used differently, AI can supply unfamiliar starting points, expose a creator to alternatives, and help someone who is stuck begin exploring.

The distinction is whether AI becomes the source of the final idea or a springboard for ideas the person develops further.

6. “AI makes mistakes, so it makes users ignorant” confuses a tool with an authority

AI can present false information in fluent, confident language. That is a real limitation, especially in medicine, science, law, and other high-stakes settings. An answer that sounds certain is not evidence that it is true.

But the existence of errors does not make every use of the tool harmful. It changes how the tool must be used. A scientist tests a measurement. A programmer tests code. A responsible AI user checks important claims against reliable sources and treats uncertainty as a reason to verify, not a reason to believe.

The relevant skill is not blind trust or total rejection. It is calibrated trust: knowing what to delegate, what to check, and when a mistake would matter.

7. The overlooked fact: AI can make more of the world reachable

For decades, determining the three-dimensional structure of proteins was slow and difficult. In 2022, researchers made more than 200 million AI-predicted protein structures available through the AlphaFold database, covering nearly all proteins catalogued by science at the time. Explore the database.

This did not mean AI had completed biology. Predictions still need interpretation and experimental work. It meant scientists gained a resource at a scale that would have been extraordinarily difficult to produce using traditional methods alone.

This is the possibility people miss when they focus only on the tasks AI might take away. AI may help people examine more ideas, build more prototypes, learn subjects that once felt inaccessible, and investigate questions that were previously too expensive to pursue.

When experimentation becomes cheaper, more ideas can meet reality. Most will fail. Some will teach us why. A few may reveal something new.

AI does not only make old work faster. It can make new work possible.

What the arguments actually show

The evidence supports a more precise conclusion than either “AI will make us dumb” or “AI can only make us smarter.” If a tool replaces practice, the skill being practised can suffer. If it provides useful explanations, feedback, and new ways to experiment, it can expand learning and capability. The long-term effects depend on the task, the design, the learner, and the way the tool is used.

We should keep practising the skills we need to own. We should ask AI for hints before solutions, verify important claims, explain ideas in our own words, and occasionally solve a fresh problem without assistance. These habits keep the human involved in the learning process.

But we should stop treating unaided effort as the only measure of intelligence. No one calls a scientist less intelligent for using a microscope. We judge the scientist by what they observe, understand, test, and discover with it.

Writing allowed ideas to outlive their authors. Printing let those ideas travel. Computers made complex calculations and simulations practical. AI adds new ways to explain, generate, test, and develop ideas.

Human progress has never depended on doing everything alone. It has depended on extending what a human mind can reach.

The goal is not to let AI do all our thinking. It is to use AI to take our thinking further. That is not a guarantee of progress. It is a possibility worth building toward.
