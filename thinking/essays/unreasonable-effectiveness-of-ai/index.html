The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

We have mistaken doing everything ourselves for being intelligent. The history of human progress tells a far more interesting story.

In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing will weaken memory and create the appearance of wisdom. More than two thousand years later, we can still read his warning because writing preserved it.

One of humanity's oldest fears about technology survived because the technology worked.

1. The phone number you forgot

You memorized your best friend's phone number. Years later, you can still recite it. Someone else saved it in their phone and forgot it almost immediately.

One person remembers the number. The other can still call their friend. The difference is real, but it is a difference in one memorized fact, not proof that one mind is more intelligent.

The phone has taken over the job of remembering those digits. It has not taken over the person's ability to understand a new idea, notice a pattern, or solve a hard problem.

We do this constantly. Calendars remember our appointments. Books store ideas. Search engines retrieve facts. These tools change what we need to keep in our heads. They also let us spend our limited attention elsewhere.

Forgetting a phone number is not the same as forgetting how to think.

2. Writing was once the dangerous new technology

Plato's warning was specific: people who relied on writing might stop training their memories and mistake access to information for wisdom. That concern sounds remarkably familiar today.

Yet writing allowed knowledge to outlive its creator. A scientist no longer has to learn only from people they can meet. They can build on discoveries made centuries earlier, by people speaking other languages, in places they will never visit.

Humanity's knowledge now exceeds what any one human brain could ever contain. Progress depends on that fact. We do not ask every scientist to rediscover physics from the beginning. We let each generation start further ahead.

Writing was supposed to weaken memory. Instead, it helped humanity build a memory larger than any human life.

Books did not make every reader wise. Some people can read without understanding a word. But the possibility of misuse never erased the power of the tool.

3. AI makes the impossible attemptable

For decades, determining the three-dimensional shape of a single protein could take years of scientific work and cost hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Then AlphaFold, an AI system, helped predict protein structures at remarkable accuracy. In 2022, researchers made more than 200 million predicted protein structures available to the scientific community, covering nearly all proteins catalogued by science at the time. [Explore the AlphaFold database](https://www.alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/).

This is more than a machine answering a question. It is a machine helping scientists examine a part of biology at a scale that would have been extraordinarily difficult to reach by traditional means alone.

The human scientists still matter. They decide what to investigate, test predictions, interpret evidence, and turn findings into discoveries. AI changes how much of the landscape they can see.

One of AI's greatest gifts may be making questions affordable to investigate that were once too expensive to ask.

4. AI can help people learn more, not merely do less

A badly used AI gives a student the answer and ends the lesson. A well-designed AI tutor explains an idea, notices confusion, offers a hint, and keeps practising with the student until understanding improves.

This is not just a nice possibility. In a 2025 randomized study, students using a purpose-built AI physics tutor achieved more than twice the median learning gains of students in the comparison classroom for the lessons tested. The result does not prove every AI tutor works, but it shows what thoughtful design can make possible. [Read the study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-97652-6).

The distinction is simple. AI can help us avoid learning, or it can give us better conditions in which to learn.

A student can copy a solution and gain nothing. Another can ask for a hint, test their reasoning, find a mistake, and solve the next problem alone. The technology is the same. The interaction is different.

5. The time we save can become the work we never tried

In a 2023 experiment involving 453 college-educated professionals doing writing tasks, access to ChatGPT cut average completion time by 40% and increased the measured quality of the work by 18%. These figures describe the tasks in that experiment, not every job or every person. [Read the study in Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586).

Speed is useful. But the deeper possibility comes after the task is finished.

A researcher might use the saved time to test another hypothesis. A programmer might prototype an idea that never justified a week of work. A student might explore a subject beyond the syllabus. A person with an idea but limited technical skills might build a first version and discover what needs improving.

When experimentation becomes cheaper, more ideas can meet reality. Most will fail. Failure is not wasted if it tells us something. A few attempts may reveal something genuinely new.

AI does not only make old work faster. It can make new work possible.

6. The mind is not required to work alone

A modern scientific discovery may depend on many researchers, years of published work, specialist instruments, software, and shared data. No one person holds every fact or performs every operation. Human intelligence has always worked through systems larger than an individual brain.

AI adds a new kind of collaborator to that system. It can draft code, compare explanations, translate information, propose approaches, and help test an idea. The human supplies direction and judgment; the tool supplies useful possibilities at speed.

This partnership is not automatically wise. AI can be confidently wrong. People still need to verify evidence, understand important decisions, and practise skills they cannot afford to lose. But those are reasons to learn how to use AI well, not reasons to dismiss what the partnership can accomplish.

The real measure of intelligence

We often measure intelligence by the work a person can perform unaided. That matters, especially when building foundational skills. It is not the whole story.

We should also notice what people can understand with good teaching, discover with powerful instruments, and accomplish by combining their judgment with tools. No one calls a scientist less intelligent for using a microscope. We celebrate what the microscope lets science observe.

AI deserves the same serious evaluation. It can produce shallow work and amplify mistakes. It can also help people learn, explore, create, and investigate at a scale they could not easily reach alone.

The goal is not to let AI do all our thinking. The goal is to use it to take our thinking further.

Human progress has never depended on doing everything alone. It has depended on extending what a human mind can reach.

Writing let our ideas outlive us. The internet connected those ideas across the world. AI may help more people turn those ideas into experiments, discoveries, and working creations.

That is not the end of human intelligence. It is a chance to give it a wider field.
