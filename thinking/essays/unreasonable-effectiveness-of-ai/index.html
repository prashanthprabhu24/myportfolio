The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

We keep mistaking the things we no longer need to do ourselves for abilities we have lost. The history of human progress suggests a more interesting possibility.

Imagine someone from a thousand years ago watching you live for a day. You need to travel to a place you have never visited, so you open a map. You need to multiply two large numbers, so you use a calculator. You cannot remember a fact, so you search for it. You want to speak to someone on another continent, so you tap a piece of glass in your pocket.

Would they conclude that you are less intelligent because you cannot calculate as quickly as a calculator, memorize every road, or recall every fact? Or would they be astonished by what an ordinary person can now do?

Now we have AI, and the old accusation has returned. It will make us lazy. It will weaken our minds. We will forget how to think.

I think this fear begins with a mistake: we have confused intelligence with doing everything unaided. But human history is not a story of minds getting better at doing everything alone. It is a story of minds building tools, sharing knowledge, and reaching beyond what one brain can manage.

1. The phone number you forgot, and the mind you did not lose

Imagine you memorized your best friend's phone number years ago. You could still recite it today. Someone else saved the number in their phone and forgot it almost immediately.

Who has the better memory? For that particular number, clearly the first person. But does that tell us who is better at understanding a new idea, solving a difficult problem, learning a language, or recognizing a pattern? Not at all.

The second person has outsourced one small job to a device. Their memory for that number may be weaker, while their ability to contact people is just as good. The tool has changed what they need to remember, not necessarily what their mind is capable of understanding.

Psychologists call this kind of behavior cognitive offloading: using the world around us to reduce the mental work we must do internally. We write reminders, use calendars, save contacts, and search the web. We do not remember every appointment because a calendar remembers it for us.

In a 2011 study known as Google Effects on Memory, researchers found evidence that people were more likely to remember where information could be found when they expected it to remain accessible. The surprising part is not simply that we forget. It is that memory can adapt to the information environment around us.

Of course, offloading has a cost. If we never practise recalling something, we may not be able to recall it when the tool is unavailable. But forgetting a phone number is not the same as losing the ability to think. We should measure what a person can do, not just what they can recite from memory.

2. Every generation fears that the next tool will weaken the mind

Writing was once criticized for weakening memory. In Plato's dialogue Phaedrus, Socrates tells a story about the invention of writing and worries that people may rely on written marks instead of exercising their memory.

There was a real trade-off. A person who writes everything down may not memorize every word. But writing gave ideas a life beyond the person who first thought them. A discovery could travel across centuries. One scholar could build on another's work without having to meet them, hear their lectures, or memorize their entire lifetime of knowledge.

Then came printing, libraries, calculators, computers, and the internet. Each made some old effort less necessary. Each also made new kinds of work possible.

Here is the extraordinary consequence: no scientist today needs to memorize all of physics before making a contribution. They can begin with centuries of recorded knowledge and push one small boundary further. Humanity can know more than any individual human being can hold in their head.

That is what tools do at their best. They let knowledge accumulate outside a single mind, so a mind can begin somewhere further ahead.

We did not become less human when our thoughts outgrew our memories. We became capable of building on thoughts no single memory could contain.

3. AI is different, and that is exactly why it matters

A notebook stores what we write. A calculator performs the operation we specify. A search engine points us toward information. AI can take part in the steps between a question and an answer.

Ask it to explain a neural network, and it can change the explanation when the first one fails. Ask it to help build a program, and it can draft code, explain an error, and propose a test. Give it a dataset, and it may help explore patterns worth investigating. It can work with us through several rounds of questions, corrections, and new ideas.

This does not mean it is always right, or that it understands things as a person does. It can invent facts and produce code that looks correct but fails. The important difference is that AI is not only a place to store knowledge. It can help transform knowledge into something useful.

Imagine a student who is stuck on a mathematical idea at eleven at night. The textbook is not making sense, and the teacher is unavailable. An AI tutor can explain the idea with a drawing, then a numerical example, then an analogy, then a practice problem. The student can keep asking until the confusion becomes clear.

That kind of patient, personalized help has historically been expensive or difficult to access. AI could make it available to far more people. It will not guarantee that everyone learns, but it changes who can get help and how many attempts they can make.

4. The same AI can make one person weaker and another stronger

A student asks AI to solve a problem, copies the answer, and submits it. The work is finished. The student may have learned almost nothing.

Another student tries first, asks for a hint, checks the reasoning, finds a mistake, and solves a similar problem without help. Both students used AI. One used it to avoid thinking. The other used it to think better.

This difference is not a footnote. It is the centre of the debate.

Learning often requires effort. Trying to recall an answer can strengthen memory more than simply rereading it. Working through a problem can reveal exactly where understanding breaks down. If AI removes every challenge, it may also remove the practice that builds skill.

But AI can also create better practice. It can offer hints instead of solutions, ask the learner to explain a step, generate new problems, and point out a misconception. A 2025 randomized study of an AI-powered physics tutor reported better learning in less time than the comparison classroom under the conditions tested.

The lesson is not that AI automatically makes us smarter. It is that AI can be designed either to bypass learning or to support it. Saying that AI makes people less intelligent because some people copy its answers is like saying books make people ignorant because some people never read beyond the first page.

5. The mind-blowing possibility: more ideas become testable

Consider a researcher with an idea for a new neural network. The idea might take ten minutes to imagine, but weeks to implement, debug, test, and compare. The researcher may abandon it, not because it is a bad idea, but because testing it costs too much time.

Now imagine AI helping write the first implementation, explaining errors, generating tests, and comparing alternatives. The researcher still has to judge whether the idea makes sense and whether the results are real. But the cost of trying it has fallen.

When experiments become cheaper, more experiments become possible. Most will fail. Some will teach us why an idea was wrong. A few may reveal something nobody expected.

That is how the frontier moves. Not every new idea is a breakthrough. Progress often comes from being able to test more possibilities, discard weak ones, and improve the promising ones.

AI could help a student build a learning app, a scientist explore a hypothesis, a small business analyse its data, or a programmer prototype a tool that previously required a team. It lowers the distance between having an idea and testing whether the idea works.

The real gain may not be that AI gives us answers faster. It may be that it lets us ask questions we previously could not afford to pursue.

6. Human intelligence has never been just one human

Think about a modern scientific discovery. It may depend on a researcher, a laboratory team, a microscope, analysis software, published papers, and decades of earlier work. No single person contains all the knowledge or performs every operation involved.

In 1998, philosophers Andy Clark and David Chalmers published The Extended Mind, exploring whether tools outside the brain can sometimes function as parts of a person's thinking. A notebook can serve as external memory. A computer can help manipulate relationships too complicated to calculate by hand.

AI makes the idea more vivid. It can help generate a candidate explanation, transform code, compare approaches, and respond when we challenge its answer. A person can use the response to ask a sharper question, which leads to another response, and then a better question.

Neither side needs to do everything. The human brings goals, context, judgment, and responsibility. The AI may contribute speed, breadth, and possible approaches. The human tests the result and decides what matters.

We already understand that a team can do more than one person alone. We understand that a telescope lets us see what our eyes cannot. It should not be surprising that a human working with AI may be able to solve problems that neither could solve independently.

7. Why we notice the danger more easily than the opportunity

It is easy to picture a student copying an essay or a programmer accepting broken code. The harm is visible. It is harder to see the student who finally understands calculus after asking twenty questions, or the researcher who tests an idea that would otherwise have remained in a notebook.

There is also a measurement problem. If AI writes a program and the program works, we have measured the success of the combined process. We have not proved that the person became a better programmer. If we want to measure learning, we should ask the person to explain the code, modify it, debug it, and solve a new problem later.

We should distinguish between doing a task, learning a skill, and extending what a person can accomplish. These are different outcomes. AI can improve one without improving all three.

That is a reason to be precise, not pessimistic. We should preserve the skills people need, teach them to verify AI's work, and design systems that reward understanding rather than mere completion. We should not confuse a badly designed use of a tool with the limits of the tool itself.

8. What if intelligence is about reach, not just effort?

Suppose one person can solve a problem alone. Another person, using AI, can solve that problem and then explore nine related questions in the same amount of time. The first person may have demonstrated more unaided effort. The second may have explored a much larger space of possibilities.

Which one is more intelligent? There is no single answer, because the word can refer to different abilities. But if our goal is to discover something new, the number of useful questions we can investigate matters.

AI will not make every attempt valuable. It can help people generate nonsense faster, just as easily as it can help them explore worthwhile ideas. More output is not automatically more progress. Human judgment becomes more important, not less, when producing possible answers becomes cheap.

Yet imagine what happens when a curious person can access an explanation, a coding partner, a translator, a research assistant, and a patient tutor in one place. The person still needs to decide what to learn and how to judge the result. But fewer ideas have to die simply because the person lacks the time or the technical skill to take the first step.

For much of history, opportunity depended heavily on where you were born, whom you knew, which books you could access, and whether you could afford a teacher. AI will not erase those inequalities. Access, quality, and cost still matter. But it could make intellectual tools available to people who were previously shut out of them.

Conclusion: We do not have to think alone to think well

The fear that AI is making us less intelligent often starts with a narrow test: can we still do every task without help? But that is not the only measure of a mind. We should also ask what it can understand, what it can discover, and what it can accomplish when supported by the world it has built.

Yes, we can use AI to avoid thinking. We can copy answers, neglect practice, and mistake fluency for truth. Those are real risks. But we can also use it to learn a difficult idea, test a new hypothesis, build a tool, or explore a problem that once seemed beyond our reach.

Writing allowed ideas to outlive their authors. Printing allowed them to travel further. Computers let us calculate and simulate what would overwhelm unaided effort. The internet connected us to knowledge across the world. Each changed what a person could do, and what humanity could build together.

AI is different because it can participate in the work of explaining, creating, and exploring. That makes it powerful, imperfect, and worth learning to use well.

Perhaps intelligence is not the ability to do everything alone. Perhaps it is the ability to discover what is possible, decide what matters, and bring together the means to make it real.

If AI helps more people learn difficult things, test more ideas, and turn curiosity into discovery, then it has not made human intelligence obsolete. It has given human intelligence a wider field in which to work.

And that possibility is far more interesting than the claim that we are simply becoming less intelligent.
