The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

The fear that AI will make us stupid takes real risks and stretches them into a much larger claim. The more interesting story is how tools change what a human mind can learn, test, and achieve.

In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing may weaken memory and create the appearance of wisdom without the substance. More than two thousand years later, we can still read that warning because writing preserved it.

The fear has survived longer than most technologies. So has the technology.

Today the accusation is aimed at AI. We are told it will make us stupid, erase our ability to think, and leave us helpless without a chatbot. Some of the evidence is worrying. But the argument often leaps from a specific skill that might weaken to a sweeping verdict on human intelligence. Those are not the same claim.

1. The phone number we forgot

Ten years ago, many of us could recite our friends' phone numbers. Today, we may struggle to remember even our own family's numbers.

Tell someone over forty that you no longer remember mobile numbers and you may get a full civilizational diagnosis: smartphones have made young people stupid. A missing string of digits has become proof that an entire generation's minds are deteriorating.

The old numbers were rehearsed every time you called someone. Saved numbers simply sat in the phone, seldom retrieved and therefore seldom strengthened.

Memory is not a cupboard where facts sit unchanged. Recall is a form of practice. Research on retrieval shows that trying to bring information back to mind can improve long-term retention, especially when practice is spaced over time. The retrieval-practice study explains why learning techniques such as spaced repetition work.

A well-known 2011 study, Google Effects on Memory, found that people sometimes remembered where information could be found rather than the information itself when they expected it to remain accessible. Read the study.

That is a change in memory strategy. It does not establish a general loss of intelligence. We stopped rehearsing phone numbers because a device could store them more reliably than we could. The digits faded. The ability to learn, reason, imagine, and remember information we still use did not thereby vanish.

2. The call that made the problem real

A friend of mine called one day to ask whether I had a Claude subscription. She had been using Claude for about a year while developing an application with her team. They had built features with it, but the team kept exhausting its shared tokens. Because of the cost, the company cancelled the subscription.

Without it, she was struggling to continue. She knew the APIs, what they accepted, and what they returned. But she was no longer sure which files implemented different parts of the application or how those pieces connected. She had worked on the same application for a year. Now she was considering paying for the tool herself.

She was a good engineer. The application existed. The features had shipped. Nothing about the story says she had become stupid overnight. Something subtler had happened: she had been able to move through the work without building a complete mental map of all the code being produced around her.

The tool had helped her get things done. It had also made it easier to leave parts of the system unexplored. As long as Claude was available, that gap was less visible. The moment it disappeared, the gap became part of her working day.

I keep coming back to this call because it is more revealing than the usual phone-number argument. The issue was not that my friend forgot trivia. It was that the map she needed to navigate her own work had become incomplete.

A 2026 review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences discusses the risks of cognitive offloading, including weaker skill acquisition and skill decay, while treating broader changes in basic cognitive ability as a separate and unresolved claim. Read the review.

That is the risk worth taking seriously. AI can let a person cross ground they have not fully learned to navigate. The question is what happens when the tool is unavailable, the situation changes, or the answer it gave was subtly wrong.

3. The verification paradox

We may use AI to skip the practice that builds expertise, then depend on that same expertise to catch the AI's mistakes.

In medicine, the consequences are not theoretical. A 2025 observational study at four Polish colonoscopy centres found that the adenoma detection rate in procedures performed without AI fell from 28.4% before AI was introduced to 22.4% after clinicians had begun using it. Adenomas can become colorectal cancers. The six percentage-point decline is concerning, but the observational study cannot establish that AI alone caused it. Read the study.

A separate 2026 prospective trial followed 13 endoscopists through 5,013 colonoscopies before, during, and after AI assistance. It found no statistically significant evidence of deskilling after AI was removed. The studies differ in design and setting. Together, they show that deskilling is a serious empirical question, not a result we should declare settled in advance. Read the trial.

A 2025 Microsoft Research survey of 319 knowledge workers collected 936 examples of AI use. Greater confidence in AI was associated with less self-reported critical-thinking effort. Workers also described a shift from generating answers toward checking and integrating what AI produced. The survey maps reported behaviour, not permanent loss of critical-thinking ability. Read the CHI study.

A separate MIT Media Lab preprint studied people writing essays with an LLM, a search engine, or no tools. It involved 54 participants, and only 18 completed the fourth session. It reported differences in EEG connectivity during the task. The work was preliminary and does not prove brain damage or long-term loss of intelligence. It does raise a question about what happens when the machine produces so much of the work that the writer engages with less of it. Read the preprint.

Now return to software. A developer can accept generated code for a data pipeline, database query, and retry mechanism without tracing how they fit together. Months later, production traffic triggers a retry loop that amplifies an outage. The developer must debug a system whose behaviour they never fully mapped. Generating the code took minutes. Reconstructing its logic at three in the morning can take hours.

Verification is not a button pressed at the end. Expertise helps us know which assumptions look suspicious and which tests would expose a hidden failure. Without the practice that builds that intuition, human oversight can become a formality.

The answer is not to make every task difficult forever. It is to protect the practice that gives us judgment, and to stop confusing a working result with a person who understands how it works.

4. Learning needs friction. It does not need every obstacle.

A student who asks AI for a solution and copies it may finish the assignment without learning the method. That is not a hypothetical problem. In a 2025 randomized study on learning AI concepts, students who used ChatGPT as a study aid scored 57.5% on a retention test 45 days later, compared with 68.5% among students who studied without it. Only 85 of the 120 enrolled participants completed the delayed test, so the result needs to be read with that limitation in view. Read the study.

In another 2025 randomized study, students learning introductory physics with a purpose-built AI tutor achieved more than double the median learning gains of an active-learning classroom comparison, while spending less time on the lesson. The tutor was designed to teach rather than hand over answers. Read the study.

Both findings can be true. An AI system that removes retrieval and practice can leave a learner with a temporary feeling of understanding. A tutor that asks for reasoning, provides hints, and responds to misconceptions can make good practice easier to access.

Students do not need to struggle with every obstacle a textbook, a timetable, or a lack of help happens to put in front of them. They do need to practise the mental operations they are expected to learn. AI should remove needless barriers without quietly removing the learning itself.

5. What this looks like in my machine learning work

In my AlexNet-style ImageNet experiment, a large model trained for hours while accuracy sat around 0.1%, roughly chance performance for a thousand-class task. The temptation is to blame the architecture, adjust the learning rate, or rewrite the training loop. But a model can run perfectly and still learn nothing useful if its targets do not mean what we think they mean.

One of the first things to investigate in a case like this is the path from image to label. Training and validation may use different class-to-index mappings. The target encoding may not match the loss function. Preprocessing can distort the image. A sophisticated network does not rescue a broken data pipeline.

This is a place where AI can multiply engineering effort. Ask it to lay out likely failure modes and draft focused checks: print image-label pairs, compare class maps, inspect output and target shapes, verify the loss convention, and see whether the model can overfit a tiny batch. Each test narrows the possibilities.

The value is not in letting a chatbot replace the training loop with a mysterious new one. It is in turning a vague symptom into a sequence of testable explanations. The outputs decide which explanation survives. A plausible suggestion that contradicts the data is still wrong.

This is the kind of AI use I want in technical work: faster exploration, more hypotheses examined, and clearer tests, while the engineer remains responsible for understanding why the system behaves as it does.

6. More output is not automatically more expertise

A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 programming studies found a moderate positive effect of generative AI on developer productivity, but no statistically significant overall effect on learning outcomes. Read the meta-analysis.

That distinction helps explain my friend's call. A team can ship more features without every engineer developing a deeper understanding of the implementation. In the short term, the product may benefit. Over time, the team may become more dependent on the tool, especially if nobody budgets time to inspect, explain, and maintain the systems being generated.

The better workflow is not to reject AI for coding. Use it to draft boilerplate, explore alternatives, explain unfamiliar APIs, and generate test cases. But take ownership of the architecture, the data flow, failure modes, and the code that must still work when conditions change. In a real system, the hardest bug is often the one nobody thought to ask the AI about.

7. Creativity has an unexpected trade-off

A 2024 experiment on short-story writing found that AI-generated ideas improved the judged creativity of individual stories, especially for writers who began with lower creativity scores. The stories also became more similar to one another. Read the study in Science Advances.

AI helped people produce stronger individual work while reducing variety across the group. If everyone accepts the first polished suggestion, the culture can become more competent and less surprising at the same time.

That finding feels especially important for someone building things. AI can help a person get past the blank page, but the odd detail, the inconvenient observation, or the unpromising experiment may be exactly what gives a project its identity. A generic answer often smooths away the very feature worth pursuing.

8. When a question becomes cheap enough to ask

For decades, determining a protein's three-dimensional structure could demand years of experimental work. In 2022, AlphaFold's developers and EMBL-EBI made more than 200 million predicted protein structures freely available, covering nearly all proteins catalogued by science at the time. Explore the database.

The database did not finish biology. Predictions still need interpretation, and many scientific questions require experiments. But suddenly, researchers around the world could start with a resource that would have been extraordinarily difficult to assemble by traditional methods alone.

This is what disappears when the whole debate is reduced to forgotten phone numbers. AI can change the economics of discovery. A student can explore an idea before finding a specialist. A researcher can screen possibilities before paying for an experiment. An engineer can build a prototype to find out whether a concept deserves more work.

When testing an idea becomes cheaper, more ideas can meet reality. Most will fail. Some will teach us something. A few may open a path that nobody had the time, money, or expertise to try before.

9. AI enters an economy, not a vacuum

AI does not enter a neutral world. It enters companies with budgets, deadlines, headcount targets, and pressure to produce more at lower cost. Those incentives shape how the technology is used. A system could be designed to train a junior engineer, or to let one senior engineer produce the work that once required three people. The same capability can support learning or make learning seem like an expense.

My friend's company cancelled its Claude subscription because the team kept exhausting its shared tokens. From the company's perspective, the bill was a cost to control. From her perspective, the tool had become part of how she got work done, and the knowledge gaps left by that workflow were still there after access disappeared. Nobody needed to intend this outcome. The budget decision and the learning consequences simply belonged to different parts of the system.

The wider evidence shows why productivity gains should not be confused with shared prosperity. A June 2026 review by the International Labour Organization found that task-level productivity gains from generative AI are real but uneven, and have not yet consistently translated into measured gains in output, earnings, or employment. Read the ILO review.

A study of Danish workers and employers, revised in 2026, likewise found that AI adoption was changing the structure of work, including the creation of tasks around AI oversight and integration, without detectable average effects on earnings or recorded hours during the first two years studied. Read the study. A faster task does not automatically mean a shorter working week, better pay, more training, or more time to learn. An organization decides where the savings go.

The distribution of the gains matters too. An IMF working paper published in 2025 models how firms' incentives to automate high-wage tasks can raise returns to capital and worsen wealth inequality, even when AI increases efficiency. This is a model of possible economic mechanisms, not a prediction that inequality must rise in every country. Read the IMF paper.

These incentives reach all the way down to the individual engineer. If a company rewards only tickets closed and features shipped, time spent understanding the code can look unproductive. If AI takes over the repetitive tasks through which junior workers once learned a system, the company may save time today while thinning out its future supply of experienced engineers. And if the tool is withdrawn to cut costs, the person left with the knowledge gap may be the worker, not the organization that captured the earlier productivity.

This is not just a question of individual discipline. Employers choose which work gets automated, what counts as productivity, whether people receive time to learn, and who absorbs the cost when a new workflow fails. The person deciding how AI is deployed, the person collecting the productivity gain, and the person carrying the risk of deskilling may be three different people.

Another friend described a more exhausting version of the same problem over dinner. His startup's CEO would get an idea, ask an AI agent to build an application, and bring the result to the engineering team as though the hard part were finished. The application was buggy, sometimes barely functional, and the team was told to fix it.

The engineers could not always tell what the product was supposed to do in the first place. There was no clear specification to compare against, no dependable definition of "working," and no shared understanding of the intended behaviour. They were expected to repair a destination that had never been properly described.

It was like being handed a half-cooked egg and told to fix it without knowing whether the chef meant to make an omelette or a boiled egg. More code, and even more AI agents, could not resolve the missing intention. The team had to reverse-engineer the idea from its broken implementation, then carry the routine burden of cleaning up what looked like progress from the outside.

That is the economic distortion in miniature. The CEO gets a demo that makes the idea look real. The engineers inherit the ambiguity, integration work, and maintenance cost. The organization counts the speed of generation, while the time spent discovering what was actually meant and making it reliable can disappear from the story of productivity.

If AI is to expand human capability rather than merely intensify work, organizations will need to measure more than output. They will need to protect training, preserve opportunities for people to practise essential skills, and share the gains from productivity instead of assuming that a faster workflow automatically benefits everyone.

Intelligence with a longer reach

My friend's experience does not prove AI is making engineers less intelligent. It does show how a productive workflow can quietly become a dependency when the work gets done faster than understanding develops. The colonoscopy studies show why the risk deserves serious attention. The tutoring studies show why abandoning AI would mean ignoring real opportunities to improve how people learn.

We need to preserve the abilities that let us judge, debug, and disagree with the systems we use. That means practising important skills, testing AI's claims, and being able to explain the systems we depend on. It also means recognizing that some obstacles are merely expensive distractions from the work that matters.

Writing let ideas outlive their authors. Libraries allowed knowledge to accumulate. Computers made calculations and simulations practical at a scale unaided minds could not manage. AI can help people explore, implement, and test ideas with new speed and flexibility.

Human progress has never depended on doing everything alone. It has depended on extending what a human mind can reach.

The goal is to reach further without losing the ability to understand the ground beneath our feet.
