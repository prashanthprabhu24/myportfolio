The Unreasonable Effectiveness of AI in Human Intelligence

The fear that AI will make us stupid takes real risks and stretches them into a much larger claim. The more interesting story is how tools change what a human mind can learn, test, and achieve.

In Plato's Phaedrus, Socrates warns that writing may weaken memory and create the appearance of wisdom without the substance. More than two thousand years later, we can still read that warning because writing preserved it.

One of humanity's oldest fears about technology survived because the technology worked.

The same fear has returned with AI. Forgotten phone numbers become evidence of failing memory. Students copying answers become evidence that learning is over. Brain scans, hallucinated facts, and generic stories become evidence that our minds are being hollowed out.

These concerns are not all imaginary. But a real problem in one kind of task is being used to support a much larger verdict about human intelligence. The evidence deserves a sharper reading.

1. The phone number we forgot

Ten years ago, many of us could recite our friends' phone numbers. Today, we may struggle to remember even our own family's numbers.

The old numbers were rehearsed every time you called someone. Saved numbers simply sat in the phone, seldom retrieved and therefore seldom strengthened.

Memory is not a permanent recording. Retrieving information strengthens access to it, and spacing those retrieval attempts over time can improve long-term retention. That is one reason spaced repetition works.

The phone-number comparison measures a change in what we practise remembering. It cannot, by itself, tell us that our reasoning, imagination, ability to learn, or intelligence as a whole has declined.

Psychologists call the wider habit cognitive offloading: using tools or the environment to carry part of a mental task. Calendars remember appointments. Search engines help us find facts. Contact lists hold numbers. This frees up attention, but it also means some skills receive less exercise.

That is the first distinction worth keeping. We can lose a specific ability through disuse. It takes much more evidence to show that the mind's wider capacity has deteriorated.

2. The strongest objection: practice really can disappear

Critics are right about something important. Skills are built through practice, and practice can be lost. A student who asks AI to solve every equation may never develop fluency. A junior developer who accepts every code suggestion may never build a working mental model of the system. When the tool disappears, the missing practice becomes visible.

A 2026 review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences examines evidence that cognitive offloading can impede skill acquisition and lead to skill decay. The authors also distinguish those risks from the much broader claim that basic cognitive abilities will necessarily erode. Read the review.

This is where the argument should become more precise. If AI makes a person worse at a task they used to perform unaided, that loss matters. It may be costly or dangerous. But it does not automatically follow that all forms of intelligence are falling, or that society would be better off without the tool.

We need to know what skill is being lost, how important it is, whether it can be preserved with deliberate practice, and what new capabilities the tool makes possible. The answer may differ for mental arithmetic, language learning, software engineering, or cancer detection.

3. AI can short-circuit learning, or improve it

A completed assignment proves that an answer was produced. It says little about whether the student can explain the answer a week later.

In a 2025 randomized study on learning AI concepts, students who used ChatGPT as a study aid scored 57.5% on a retention test 45 days later, compared with 68.5% among students who studied without it. Only 85 of the 120 enrolled participants completed that delayed test, and the results concern one learning context rather than every way AI can be used. Read the study.

Now look at a different 2025 randomized study. Students using a purpose-built AI physics tutor achieved more than double the median learning gains of a classroom active-learning comparison, while spending less time on the lesson. The tutor was designed to teach, not simply reveal answers. Read the study.

These findings point to the design of the learning process. A system that gives away solutions can remove the very retrieval and problem-solving that build knowledge. A system that probes a student's reasoning, offers hints, corrects misconceptions, and creates more practice can make learning more accessible.

The technology is capable of both. The outcome depends on what the learner is asked to do, what the system rewards, and whether understanding is checked after the AI is gone.

4. The verification paradox

This is the hardest problem for anyone arguing that AI simply makes us more capable: we may use AI to skip the practice that builds expertise, then depend on that same expertise to catch the AI's mistakes.

In medicine, the consequences are not theoretical. A 2025 observational study at four Polish colonoscopy centers compared unaided procedures before and after AI-assisted detection was introduced. The adenoma detection rate in non-AI procedures fell from 28.4% to 22.4% after the introduction. That is a six percentage-point decline in finding precancerous growths. The study raises a serious warning, but its observational design cannot establish that AI caused the change. Read the study.

A 2026 prospective trial followed 13 endoscopists through 5,013 colonoscopies before, during, and after AI assistance. It found no statistically significant evidence of either skill improvement or skill loss after the system was removed. The two studies differ in design and setting, so they do not cancel each other out. Together, they show why deskilling needs careful measurement rather than confident slogans. Read the trial.

The same paradox appears in software. Imagine a developer who lets AI write every data pipeline, database query, and retry mechanism. The application works during a demonstration. Months later, production traffic triggers a race condition or a retry loop that amplifies an outage. The developer must debug a system whose pieces they never fully understood. The time saved while generating code is suddenly dwarfed by the time required to rediscover its logic.

Verification is not a button that can simply be pressed at the end. Expertise is what helps someone know where a bug might hide, which assumption is suspicious, and which test would expose a hidden failure. If AI use hollows out that expertise, human oversight becomes ceremonial.

The solution cannot be to keep every task difficult forever. It is to protect the forms of practice that create judgment, and use AI to handle work that does not need to be a learning exercise.

  • When learning a skill, attempt the problem first and request hints before complete solutions.
  • When using generated code, inspect the assumptions and add tests for edge cases, failure modes, and unexpected inputs.
  • At intervals, perform key tasks without assistance to see whether the underlying skill is still there.
  • For high-stakes decisions, require independent evidence and a human who is capable of disagreeing with the system.

These practices are not ceremonial caution. They are how a person keeps the expertise needed to use a powerful tool safely.

5. What this looks like in machine learning

In my own AlexNet-style ImageNet experiments, I trained a large model and encountered accuracy stuck around 0.1%, essentially chance performance for a thousand-class problem. After hours of computation, changing the network or lowering the learning rate would have been an easy reaction. It would also have been premature.

A failure like that can originate far from the architecture. Image files can be paired with the wrong labels. The order of class indices can disagree with the validation mapping. The loss function may expect integer class IDs while the pipeline supplies a different encoding. A preprocessing step can distort inputs. Training may run without the model learning anything useful.

This is where an AI assistant can act as a cognitive multiplier. I can ask it to help enumerate plausible failure modes and draft small diagnostic checks: print several image-label pairs, compare class-to-index maps, inspect the output and target shapes, verify the loss convention, and attempt to overfit a tiny subset. These tests turn a vague failure into a series of claims that can be checked.

The useful contribution is not that AI magically knows the bug. It can help me search more systematically and write the instrumentation faster. The data decides which hypothesis survives. If its suggestion conflicts with the actual outputs, the suggestion is discarded.

This process can improve my understanding of the training pipeline because I must connect each proposed cause to an observable result. If I simply paste in a rewritten training loop and celebrate when the metric rises, I may have repaired the symptom without learning why the system failed.

There is a difference between asking AI to think in my place and using it to help me think through more possibilities. In engineering, the second can be valuable precisely because every hypothesis must eventually face the machine.

6. The early evidence on AI and software developers

A 2026 paper in Management Science combined three randomized field experiments involving 4,867 software developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and a Fortune 100 company. Developers given AI coding assistance completed about 26% more tasks on average, though results varied among the experiments. Read the paper.

That is meaningful evidence of higher productivity. It is not evidence that developers learned 26% more or became 26% more intelligent.

A 2026 meta-analysis of 23 programming studies reached a similar distinction: a moderate positive effect on productivity, but no statistically significant overall effect on learning outcomes. The evidence suggests that AI can help people produce more without reliably ensuring that they gain more skill. Read the meta-analysis.

This is why the best engineering workflow will not delegate everything indiscriminately. Let AI accelerate boilerplate, generate test ideas, explain unfamiliar APIs, or compare implementation approaches. Keep the developer involved in architecture, assumptions, debugging, code review, and the mental model needed to maintain the system under conditions the original author did not anticipate.

7. The evidence is stranger than “AI kills creativity”

A 2024 experiment on short-story writing found that AI-generated ideas improved the rated creativity of individual stories, especially for writers who started out less creative. The same AI assistance made the stories more similar to one another. Read the study in Science Advances.

That is a genuine trade-off. AI helped more people produce stronger individual work while reducing diversity across the group. If everyone accepts the first polished suggestion, the culture may become more competent and less surprising at the same time.

The useful response is to make AI widen the search rather than narrow it. Ask for approaches that disagree. Bring your own rough idea before looking at generated options. Combine outputs with experience, evidence, and the strange details a generic model would be likely to smooth away.

Creativity has never meant producing something from nothing. It includes selection, taste, recombination, persistence, and the willingness to follow an idea after the easy suggestions run out. AI can supply more ingredients. It cannot decide which meal is worth cooking.

8. What happens when a question becomes cheap enough to ask

For decades, determining a protein's three-dimensional structure could demand years of experimental work. In 2022, AlphaFold's developers and EMBL-EBI made more than 200 million predicted protein structures freely available, covering nearly all proteins catalogued by science at the time. Explore the database.

The database did not finish biology. Predictions still need interpretation, and many scientific questions require experiments. But a resource that would once have been unimaginable became available to researchers across the world.

This is the scale of possibility that gets lost in arguments about phone numbers and homework. AI can change the economics of discovery. A student can explore an idea before finding a specialist. A researcher can screen possibilities before spending money on an experiment. A programmer can build a prototype to learn whether a concept is worth pursuing.

When the cost of testing an idea falls, more ideas can be tested. Most will fail. Some will sharpen our understanding. A few may open a door that was previously closed simply because nobody had enough time, money, or expertise to try.

That is not a guarantee that AI will make every person wiser. It is a concrete way AI can expand the territory in which human intelligence operates.

Intelligence with a longer reach

The arguments against AI often begin with something true: people can forget what they stop practising, outsource too much judgment, accept convincing errors, and lose skills they still need. These risks deserve serious safeguards, especially where lives, safety, or essential expertise are involved.

But these observations do not justify the sweeping conclusion that humanity is becoming stupid. They show that capability depends on the relationship between a person, a task, a tool, and the practice surrounding its use.

Writing let ideas outlive their authors. Libraries allowed knowledge to accumulate. Computers made calculations and simulations practical at a scale unaided minds could not manage. AI can help people explore, implement, and test ideas with new speed and flexibility.

That power makes human judgment more important, not disposable. We need people who understand enough to set direction, recognise failure, evaluate evidence, and decide which outcomes matter. We also need to stop treating unaided struggle as a virtue in every situation. Some friction teaches; some friction merely prevents people from beginning.

The important question is what happens to human capability when intelligence is no longer working alone.

AI will amplify what we build around it. If we build habits of copying, we will get dependency. If we build habits of experimentation, verification, and learning, we can turn it into leverage. The aim should be to preserve the human abilities that matter while using AI to reach problems and possibilities that previously lay beyond us.
